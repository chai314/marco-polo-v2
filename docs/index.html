Price an option by dropping balls

Nobody knows where a stock will be in six months. So we imagine thousands of possible futures, check what the option pays in each one, and take the average. That average is the price.

This little lab shows how that works. Then it shows four tricks that get the same answer with far fewer balls.

Skip to the lab

Everything here runs live in your browser on a C++ engine. Nothing is a recording.

One ball, one future

The ball starts at today’s price of SPY, 718.78. Each row of pins is a step forward in time. At every step the ball gets a random nudge, left or right, big or small. Where it lands is the stock price on expiry day, half a year from now.

Our option is a call with a strike of 719. It pays whatever the price ends above 719. If the price ends below, it pays nothing.

Drop a ball to see what it pays.

Many balls, one calm average

One ball is a wild guess. The average of many balls is calm. The needle under the board shows the average payoff so far.

The yellow band is the true price, 46.53, from the Black–Scholes formula. We only use it to check our work. The balls never see it.

With 2,000 balls the needle usually stops within ±2.4 of the truth. “Usually” means 19 times out of 20.

The catch: four times the balls, half the error

The error shrinks with the square root of the number of balls. Four times the balls only halves it. One more correct digit costs a hundred times the balls.

Try it. Each dot below is a whole experiment. Watch how slowly the cloud of answers tightens.

So let’s cheat, legally. Each trick below keeps the answer right on average. It just makes every experiment less wild. We always compare at the same number of balls, and if a trick costs extra work, we count that too.

ATrick A: mirror twins

For every ball, we drop a twin that gets the opposite nudge at every pin. When one flies far right, the other flies far left.

For a call, one twin often pays a lot while the other pays little. Their average swings much less than the average of two strangers.

This works when the payoff only moves one way as the price rises, as it does for a call or a put. Each twin costs a full ball, and we count it.

STrick S: a fair share for every lane

Random balls bunch up by luck: too many in one spot, too few in another. So we cut the landing zone into 32 lanes that are all equally likely, and send exactly the same number of balls down each one.

The luck of which lane a ball picks is gone. Only the luck inside each lane is left.

Under the hood, we fix where each path is headed overall, then fill in the day-to-day wiggles at random.

CTrick C: check against an answer key

There is one thing we know exactly: the average final price of the stock itself. So we track it on the same balls.

If the balls happen to land too high, the stock average comes out too high. The option estimate is then probably too high as well. We know the stock’s mistake exactly, so we subtract a matching share of it from the option.

That share is called β. We learn it from a small separate trial run, so the trick never tilts the answer.

KTrick K: let a formula roll the dice

Now let the stock jump. About 0.43 times a year, it drops suddenly, by 28% on a typical jump. The market’s option prices tell us to expect this.

Jumps change the true price too: the yellow band moves to 47.30, now from Merton’s formula for a jumping stock.

Here is the trick. Once we know how many jumps happen, the rest of the path is smooth, and a formula prices it exactly. So we roll dice only for the number of jumps. The formula does the rest.

For barrier options, K works differently. Instead of checking whether a ball touched the barrier, we compute the chance that it brushed it between two rows of pins.

Mix the tricks

Tricks can be combined. Sometimes their gains multiply. Sometimes they fight over the same noise: S and C both go after where the ball ends up, so together they are not twice as good.

      The fine print, in plain words

      • Balls are the engine’s real paths. The board draws paths from the same random-number generator the engine uses. The needle and every error bar come from the engine’s own estimates, not from the animation.
      • Error bars can be too small. When the spread we actually see across repeated runs is clearly bigger than the error a run reports about itself, we use the bigger one and say so. This happens with rare jumps: a short run may see none.
      • Two estimators lean slightly. American options (priced by Longstaff–Schwartz) and barriers checked only on a grid of dates are a little biased. We measure their spread around their own average and show the lean separately.
      • Speeds are rough. Timings come from one thread in your browser and are noisy.
      • Data. Prices and option quotes for SPY, QQQ, IWM, AAPL, MSFT and NVDA at the close of 24 November 2026, from Yahoo Finance; the three-month Treasury rate from FRED. You can switch to a synthetic market drawn in your browser.
      • Checks. Every price is compared with one that was not simulated: Black–Scholes, Merton’s series, a quadrature for the Asian, Reiner–Rubinstein for barriers and a lattice for the American put. The browser engine matches the native C++ build to nine digits.
